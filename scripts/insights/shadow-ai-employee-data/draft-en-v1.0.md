Walk into any Arab ministry, hospital or bank today and you will find an employee pasting text into an AI window to summarize or translate it.

It might be meeting minutes, a patient file, or a customer's account statement.

The tool is usually free, opened from the employee's personal account.

**The institution does not see it, but the institution is the one that will pay.**

## The Short Answer: A Ban on Paper Does Not Protect Data; a Safe Tool Does

**The answer:** Our employees already use AI, and many of them use it outside what their institution allows. A ban or a written policy alone does not change that. What changes it is giving staff a safe tool under a clear contract, one that is more useful than the free tool, and then training them and measuring the result.

Three messages support this answer:

1. **Use is widespread and out of the institution's sight.** More than 9 in 10 employees in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Egypt intentionally use AI at work [2][3][4]. Globally, 70% use free public tools, and only 42% use tools provided by their employer [1].
2. **A written policy is not enough.** About three in four employees in the three countries say their organization has a policy, yet 47% to 56% have broken it [2][3][4]. Globally, uploading company information was higher where AI was banned (67%) than where there was no policy (33%) [1].
3. **The cost falls on the institution and its management.** In Saudi Arabia fines reach SAR 5 million [8]. In Egypt the "person responsible for the actual management" can face the same penalties [10].

## How Many of Our Employees Use AI at Work?

In 2025 KPMG and the University of Melbourne published a survey of 48,340 people in 47 countries, including Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Egypt. Responses were collected between November 2024 and mid-January 2025 [1].

Our region scores above the global average. 92% in Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and 91% in Egypt, said they intentionally use AI at work [2][3][4].

But with which tool? Globally, most employees use free public tools (70%). And 48% said they had uploaded company information, such as sales figures or customer data, into public AI tools [1].

A "public tool" here means a service open to anyone on the internet, with no contract binding it to the institution on where data is stored and who can see it.

In short: data leaves the institution every day through a door nobody watches.

## Why Is a Written Policy Not Enough?

Most of our institutions have written a policy. But employee behaviour under work pressure is another matter.

## Exhibit 1: Three in four organizations have a policy, and close to half of employees break it

| Country | Intentionally use AI at work | Say their organization has policies for responsible use | Used AI in ways that contravene policies | Relied on AI output without checking its accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Saudi Arabia [2] | 92% | 73% | 47% | 70% |
| UAE [3] | 92% | 73% | 56% | 72% |
| Egypt [4] | 91% | 71% | 48% | 75% |
| Global average [1] | — | — | 44% | 66% |

*Source: KPMG and University of Melbourne, Trust, attitudes and use of AI 2025, country insights [2][3][4] and global report [1]. Figures are self-reported by employees; the report notes they likely understate reality because people are reluctant to admit breaches [1].*

The surprise in the global report: employees in organizations that ban generative AI were the most likely to upload company information to public tools [1].

## Exhibit 2: Where AI is banned, employees uploaded more company data, not less

| Organization's stance on generative AI | Share who uploaded company information to public tools |
|---|---|
| Bans it | 67% |
| Has a policy guiding its use | 56% |
| Employee does not know if there is a policy | 38% |
| No policy | 33% |

*Source: KPMG and University of Melbourne, global report 2025, p. 75 [1]. This is a correlation, not proven causation. The report's own reading is that outright bans "may be ineffective" and that having a policy "does not guarantee compliance" without clear guidance and education.*

IBM says 20% of the organizations in its 2025 study suffered a breach because of "shadow AI", meaning tools employees use without the organization's knowledge. Organizations with high levels of shadow AI paid on average USD 670,000 more per breach [5].

Only 37% of organizations in the study have policies to manage AI or detect shadow AI [6]. In the Middle East the average cost of a single breach was SAR 27 million, about USD 7.29 million, the second highest average after the United States [5][7].

In short: a ban pushes use into the shadows; it does not stop it.

## Who Pays If the Data Gets Out?

Here the question becomes a legal one.

In Saudi Arabia, the Personal Data Protection Law classifies "health data" as sensitive data [8]. It sets conditions for transferring data outside the Kingdom, including a level of protection no lower than the Law's and limiting transfer to the minimum necessary (Article 29) [8].

In Egypt, Law No. 151 of 2020 penalizes breaches of the cross-border transfer rules, and holds the institution's de facto manager liable if they knew and their failure contributed to the breach [10].

## Exhibit 3: The law puts the cost on the institution and its manager, not on the free tool

| Provision | What it covers | Maximum stated penalty |
|---|---|---|
| Saudi Arabia, PDPL, Article 36 [8] | Any breach of the Law or its Regulations not covered by Article 35 | Warning or fine up to SAR 5 million, doubled for repeat violations |
| Saudi Arabia, Article 35 [8] | Disclosing or publishing sensitive data with intent to harm or for personal benefit | Up to 2 years' imprisonment and/or fine up to SAR 3 million |
| Egypt, Law 151 of 2020, Article 42 [10] | Breaching the rules on cross-border movement of personal data | At least 3 months' imprisonment and/or fine of EGP 500,000 to 5 million |
| Egypt, Article 41 [10] | Transferring or circulating sensitive data without the data subject's consent | At least 3 months' imprisonment and/or fine of EGP 500,000 to 5 million |
| Egypt, Article 47 [10] | The person responsible for the actual management of the violating entity | The same penalties, if knowledge and contributing breach of duty are proven |
| Saudi Arabia, SDAIA Generative AI Guidelines for Government, section 3.5 [9] | Government employees must not enter data classified "restricted" or higher into generative AI tools | Guidance; no penalty in itself |

*Source: PDPL text published by SDAIA [8], SDAIA guidelines [9], and the text of Egyptian Law 151 of 2020 [10]. Whether pasting personal data into a foreign tool counts legally as "transfer" or "disclosure" is a legal question for each institution's counsel; we do not answer it here.*

In short: the tool is free for the employee, but it can cost the institution and its manager a great deal.

## What Did Korea Do When It Faced the Same Problem?

In April 2023 Samsung found three cases in which engineers had uploaded source code and internal meeting minutes to ChatGPT. It banned public tools on company devices from 1 May 2023 and said it was building its own tools [11][12].

About six months later Samsung announced Samsung Gauss, saying it "is currently used on employee productivity": composing emails, summarizing documents, translation and coding assistance [13].

In August 2025 Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission (PIPC) issued a guideline on using generative AI [14]. Among its recommendations for an organization using an external model: enterprise licences with robust data protection, data protection clauses in the contract, and verification of overseas data transfer [15].

In short: Korea did not stop at a ban; it put an internal alternative and a clear contract in place.

## What It Means for Decision Makers: Five Steps Starting Tomorrow

1. **Ask your employees before you ban them.** A short anonymous survey: which tools do you use, and for what tasks?
2. **Provide a safe tool that is more useful than the free one.** An enterprise licence or an internal model, under a contract that fixes where data is stored, forbids its use to train the vendor's models, and gives the institution audit rights [15].
3. **Make the rule follow data classification, not a long list.** "Public" data may go in; "restricted" or higher goes only into the approved tool, as in SDAIA's guidelines for government entities [9].
4. **Train on real cases.** A patient file, an account statement, board minutes. The global report links irresponsible use to a lack of training and guidance [1].
5. **Measure every three months.** Share of use through the approved tool, number of detected cases, and a short report to the board.

## The VisionSeek View (Opinion)

Shadow AI is not a discipline problem. It is a sign that the institution has not provided the tool its employees need.

On the ownership ladder, an institution whose staff work with free tools does not even stand on the "use" rung: it has no contract, no record, and no knowledge of where its data went.

An enterprise licence with a clear contract puts it on the "use" rung on its own terms. An internal tool built on its own data, as Samsung did, brings it closer to "adaptation".

Contract clauses that fix where data lives and grant audit rights are at the core of what our **VS-P03 Clause** programme reviews.

## Conclusion

In short: do not ask whether your staff use AI; ask which tool, and who owns what goes into it.

Questions we will watch: Will an Arab regulator start asking about employees' use of public AI tools during inspections? And will Egypt issue its first penalty over data transferred through an AI tool?

## Methodology and Sources

**Scope:** Employee use of public AI tools in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Egypt, and the personal data legal framework in Saudi Arabia and Egypt. **Out of scope:** UAE law, as we did not review its penalty provisions here (which does not mean they are absent), and sector-specific rules such as banking.

**Data:** Country figures from the country insights pages [2][3][4]. The upload rate by organizational stance is a global figure, not specific to the three countries [1].

**What we do not know:** The country pages do not report the share who uploaded company information to public tools. We found no published ruling in Saudi Arabia or Egypt concerning data pasted into an AI tool; that does not mean none exists. This report is not legal advice.

**Ownership ladder:** We do not rate any specific institution. The reading in the opinion section is general.
